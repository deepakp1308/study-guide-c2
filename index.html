C2 — Atoms, Elements and Compounds

1. Elements, Compounds and Mixtures (C2.01)

Elements

Every substance is made up of atoms — the incredibly small particles from which the whole material world is built.

  • Element — a substance made up of only one type of atom; it cannot be broken down into anything simpler by chemical reactions.
  • All the atoms of an element contain the same number of protons.
  • There are 118 known elements, and 94 of them are found naturally on Earth.
  • Most of the known mass of the universe is just two elements: hydrogen (92%) and helium (7%). All the other elements make up only 1%.
  • Just eight elements make up more than 98% of the mass of the Earth's crust. Silicon and oxygen, bound together in silicate rocks, make up almost three-quarters of it.

Metals and Non-metals

Elements can be divided into metals and non-metals. Their different properties come from their different structure and bonding (see sections 5 to 8).

Property Metals Non-metals
Position in the Periodic Table Left and centre Top right
Electrical conductivity Good conductors Poor conductors (graphite is an exception)
When a force is applied Malleable: can be bent and beaten into sheets Brittle if solid
Ions formed Lose outer electrons to form positive ions, e.g. Na⁺, Mg²⁺ Gain electrons to form negative ions, e.g. Cl⁻, O²⁻
Bonding in the element Metallic bonding Covalent bonding (the noble gases exist as single atoms)

Compounds and Mixtures

  • Compound — two or more elements chemically combined in fixed proportions; the ratio of the different types of atom is always the same.
  • Mixture — two or more elements or compounds mixed together without being chemically bonded; the amounts of each can vary.

Example — Iron and Sulfur

  • A mixture of iron and sulfur powders can contain any amounts of the two elements. Each element keeps its own properties, so the iron can be separated out with a magnet.
  • When the mixture is heated, the elements react to form a black compound, iron sulfide, which is not attracted to a magnet.

iron + sulfur → iron sulfide

2. Atomic Structure and the Periodic Table (C2.02)

The Structure of the Atom

  • Modern ideas about atoms are based on the atomic theory put forward by John Dalton in 1807: atoms are the basic building blocks of the elements, and they can join together to make molecules.
  • Later research showed that atoms are made of even smaller subatomic particles. The electron was discovered in 1897, soon followed by the proton. By 1932, when the neutron was discovered, it was clear that all atoms are made of protons, neutrons and electrons.
  • At the centre of the atom is a very small, positively charged nucleus, which contains the protons and neutrons and almost all the mass of the atom.
  • The negatively charged electrons are arranged in shells around the nucleus. Most of the atom is the space occupied by the electrons.
  • The atom is the smallest particle that shows the chemical characteristics of a particular element.

Protons, Neutrons and Electrons

Subatomic particle Relative mass Relative charge Location in atom
Proton 1 +1 In the nucleus
Neutron 1 0 In the nucleus
Electron 1/1840 (negligible) −1 Outside the nucleus, in shells
  • Protons and neutrons have almost the same mass. Electrons have virtually no mass (1/1840, or 0.00054, of the mass of a proton).
  • The electrons are held in the atom by the electrostatic force of attraction between them and the positive protons in the nucleus.
  • An atom is electrically neutral: it has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so the positive charge of the nucleus is balanced by the negative charge of the electrons.
  • Hydrogen is the only atom with no neutrons: it has 1 proton and 1 electron.
  • Helium has 2 protons, 2 neutrons and 2 electrons. Its charges balance: (+2) + (−2) = 0. Its mass is 2 + 2 = 4 units, because the electrons add almost nothing.
  • The number of neutrons needed to hold the nucleus together increases as atoms get larger. A gold atom has 79 protons, 118 neutrons and 79 electrons.

Proton Number and Mass Number

  • Proton number (atomic number), Z — the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom. It identifies the element: only hydrogen atoms have 1 proton, only helium atoms have 2 and only gold atoms have 79.
  • Mass number (nucleon number), A — the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus. Protons and neutrons together are called nucleons.
  • An atom is written with the mass number at the top left of the symbol and the proton number at the bottom left: ⁴₂He, ⁷₃Li, ¹²₆C, ¹⁶₈O, ²³⁸₉₂U.

To work out the particles in an atom:

  • Number of protons = proton number (Z)
  • Number of electrons = number of protons (Z), because the atom is neutral
  • Number of neutrons = mass number − proton number (A − Z)

Worked Example — Particles in ²³₁₁Na

  1. The proton number is 11, so there are 11 protons.
  2. The atom is neutral, so there are also 11 electrons.
  3. The mass number is 23, so the number of neutrons is 23 − 11 = 12 neutrons.

Electronic Configuration

In 1913 Niels Bohr, working with Ernest Rutherford, developed a theory of how the electrons are arranged in atoms:

  • Electrons orbit the nucleus in electron shells (energy levels), which have different energies.
  • The shells are filled starting with the one with the lowest energy, closest to the nucleus. Shells further from the nucleus have higher energies.
  • The first shell can hold only 2 electrons.
  • The second shell holds up to 8 electrons. For the first 20 elements the third shell also holds up to 8; in later elements it can expand to hold 18.
  • Full shells are the most stable arrangement.

The electronic configuration gives the number of electrons in each shell, starting from the innermost shell, e.g. sodium is 2,8,1.

Worked Example — Electronic Configuration of Aluminium

  1. Aluminium has proton number 13, so the atom has 13 electrons.
  2. The first shell takes 2 electrons, leaving 11.
  3. The second shell takes 8 electrons, leaving 3.
  4. The remaining 3 electrons go into the third shell, so the configuration is 2,8,3.
  • When compounds of some metals are heated in a flame, electrons in the atoms move between two different shells and light of a characteristic colour is given out. This is the basis of flame tests; the aurora borealis is caused in a similar way, by radiation from the Sun moving the electrons in atoms of the gases in the atmosphere.

Electronic Configuration and the Periodic Table

  • Group number = the number of electrons in the outer shell.
  • Period number = the number of occupied shells.
  • Example: silicon is in Group IV, Period 3, so it has 4 outer electrons and 3 shells: 2,8,4.
  • Working backwards: an atom with the configuration 2,8,7 has 7 outer electrons and 3 shells, so it is in Group VII, Period 3. It has 2 + 8 + 7 = 17 electrons, so Z = 17: it is chlorine.

The Noble Gas Electronic Configuration

  • The noble gases (Group VIII) are very unreactive gases.
  • Their atoms have a full outer shell of electrons — 8 outer electrons (helium has 2) — which is a very stable arrangement.
  • Noble gas atoms do not form bonds with other atoms by sharing or transferring electrons, so they exist as separate atoms.
  • Atoms of other elements react by losing, gaining or sharing outer electrons so that they end up with a noble gas electronic configuration.

3. Isotopes (C2.03)

What Are Isotopes?

  • Isotopes — atoms of the same element that have the same proton number but a different nucleon (mass) number.
  • The difference comes from different numbers of neutrons in the nucleus; the numbers of protons and electrons are the same.
  • Isotopes are named using their mass number, e.g. carbon-12, carbon-13 and carbon-14. Their symbols show both numbers, e.g. ¹²₆C and ¹⁴₆C.
  • Pure samples of many elements, such as hydrogen, carbon and chlorine, naturally contain a mixture of isotopes.

Properties of Isotopes

  • Isotopes of an element have the same chemical properties because they have the same number of electrons, and so the same electronic configuration. It is the number of electrons in an atom that decides how it forms bonds and reacts with other atoms.
  • Some physical properties of isotopes are different, e.g. their mass, and some isotopes are radioactive.

4. Chemical Bonding (C2.04)

Elements Combine to Make Compounds

  • Each compound is made of particular elements, always combined in the same proportions. For example, each water molecule contains two hydrogen atoms bonded to one oxygen atom.
  • When elements react to form a compound, their characteristic properties are replaced by those of the compound. The properties of a compound bear no direct relationship to those of the elements that make it up.
Substance Properties
Sodium A highly reactive metal; fizzes violently with water and may even cause a flame
Chlorine A toxic gas, once used as a chemical weapon and still used to kill harmful microbes in water treatment
Sodium chloride (common salt) A harmless crystalline solid, used to flavour food and essential for life

Burning sodium in chlorine gas produces a white smoke of sodium chloride:

sodium + chlorine → sodium chloride

2Na(s) + Cl₂(g) → 2NaCl(s)

Chemical Formulae

  • A chemical formula is a shorthand way of representing elements and compounds using the symbols of the elements.
  • The formula shows the ratio of atoms in the compound: NaCl has 1 sodium to 1 chlorine, and H₂O has 2 hydrogen atoms to 1 oxygen atom.

Types of Chemical Bonding

  • Chemical bonding — the strong forces that hold atoms (or ions) together in a substance. Whatever the type of bonding, it is always the outer electrons of the atoms that are involved.
  • Molecular compounds — atoms bonded together by shared electrons, e.g. water, ammonia and methane.
  • Ionic compounds — many ions (charged particles) held together in a regular structure by the attraction between their opposite charges, e.g. sodium chloride.

Metal + non-metal(s) Ionic: electrons transferred Giant ionic lattice NaCl, MgO
Non-metal elements, and non-metal + non-metal(s) Covalent: electrons shared Simple molecules or giant molecular lattices Cl₂, H₂O, CH₄; diamond, SiO₂

5. Ions and Ionic Bonds (C2.05)

Formation of Ions

  • Ion — a charged particle formed when an atom (or a group of atoms) loses or gains electrons.
  • Most atoms do not have a full outer shell. One way to gain the stable noble gas configuration is to transfer electrons completely from one atom to another.
  • Cation — a positive ion, formed when a metal atom loses its outer electrons. The charge equals the number of electrons lost.
  • Anion — a negative ion, formed when a non-metal atom gains electrons. The charge equals the number of electrons gained.

A sodium atom (Group I) loses its one outer electron:

Na (2,8,1) → Na⁺ (2,8) + e⁻

The sodium ion has a single positive charge because it has only 10 electrons but still has 11 protons in its nucleus. It now has a complete outer shell, the stable configuration of neon.

A chlorine atom (Group VII) gains one electron:

Cl (2,8,7) + e⁻ → Cl⁻ (2,8,8)

The chloride ion has a single negative charge because it has one more electron (18) than there are protons in its nucleus (17). It now has the configuration of argon.

Ionic Bonding

Ionic bonding occurs in compounds of metals with non-metals. Electrons are transferred from the metal atoms to the non-metal atoms, and the oppositely charged ions formed are held together by strong electrostatic forces of attraction. This is the ionic bond.

The important features of ionic bonding are:

  • The electrons involved in forming the ions are those in the outer shell of the atoms.
  • Metal atoms lose their outer electrons to become positive ions. The charge equals the number of electrons lost.
  • Generally, non-metal atoms gain electrons to become negative ions. The charge equals the number of electrons gained.
  • The ions formed have the stable electron arrangement of the nearest noble gas in the Periodic Table.

Dot-and-Cross Diagrams for Ionic Compounds

In a dot-and-cross diagram, the electrons of one atom are drawn as dots and those of the other atom as crosses, usually showing the outer electrons only. An arrow shows the electron transfer, and each ion formed is drawn in square brackets with its charge at the top right.

Compound Electron transfer Ions formed Formula
Sodium chloride Na (2,8,1) gives its 1 outer electron to Cl (2,8,7) Na⁺ (2,8) and Cl⁻ (2,8,8) NaCl
Magnesium oxide Mg (2,8,2) gives its 2 outer electrons to O (2,6) Mg²⁺ (2,8) and O²⁻ (2,8) MgO
Calcium chloride Ca (2,8,8,2) gives 1 electron to each of two Cl atoms (2,8,7) Ca²⁺ (2,8,8) and two Cl⁻ (2,8,8) CaCl₂
  • In the drawing of the ions, the metal ion has an empty outer shell because its outer electrons have gone. The non-metal ion has 8 outer electrons: its own dots plus the transferred cross or crosses.
  • In magnesium oxide, both ions have the configuration of neon; in calcium chloride, all the ions have the configuration of argon.
  • The formula follows from the charges, because the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge: Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ give MgO, while Ca²⁺ needs two Cl⁻ ions, giving CaCl₂.

Physical Properties of Ionic Compounds

In the solid, the ions are arranged in a giant ionic lattice: a regular arrangement in which each ion is surrounded by ions of the opposite charge. The whole structure is held together by the electrostatic forces of attraction between the oppositely charged ions.

Property of ionic compounds Reason
Solids at room temperature There is a regular arrangement of the ions in a lattice; ions with opposite charges are next to each other
High melting and boiling points The ions are attracted to each other by strong electrostatic forces; large amounts of energy are needed to separate them
Often soluble in water (not usually soluble in organic solvents such as ethanol or methylbenzene) Water is attracted to charged ions, so many ionic solids dissolve in water
Conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water, but not when solid In the liquid or solution the ions are free to move towards the electrodes when a voltage is applied; in the solid they are held in fixed positions

6. Simple Molecules and Covalent Bonds (C2.06)

Covalent Bonding

  • Most non-metal elements are made of molecules, not separate atoms; only the noble gases exist as individual atoms. Hydrogen (H₂), oxygen (O₂), nitrogen (N₂) and chlorine (Cl₂) consist of diatomic molecules.
  • Covalent bonding — bonding formed by the sharing of one or more pairs of electrons between atoms. It is the bonding in non-metal elements and in compounds of one non-metal with another.
  • A covalent bond is held together by the attraction between the shared pair of electrons (negative) and the nuclei (positive) of the two atoms.

The main features of covalent bonding are:

  • The bond is formed by the sharing of a pair of electrons between two atoms, leading to noble gas electronic configurations.
  • Generally, each atom contributes an equal number of electrons to each bond (one electron each for a single bond).

Other key points:

  • Hydrogen atoms gain a share of 2 electrons (like helium); atoms of other elements gain a share of 8 outer electrons.
  • A single bond is one shared pair (drawn as a line, –), a double bond is two shared pairs (=) and a triple bond is three shared pairs (≡). These lines are used in displayed formulae.
  • Dot-and-cross diagrams show the outer electrons only, because the inner electrons are not involved in the bonding.
  • The number of covalent bonds an atom usually forms equals the number of electrons it needs to complete its outer shell: H 1, Cl 1, O 2, N 3, C 4.

Physical Properties of Simple Covalent Compounds

There are two levels of force in a simple molecular substance:

  • The covalent bonds within each molecule are strong and difficult to break.
  • The intermolecular forces between the molecules are only weak and are easily broken.

When a simple molecular substance melts or boils, only the weak intermolecular forces are overcome; the covalent bonds do not break. So only a small amount of energy is needed to let the molecules move away from each other.

Property of simple covalent compounds Reason
Often liquids or gases at room temperature They are made of simple molecules; the atoms are joined together by covalent bonds
Low melting and boiling points The forces between the molecules (intermolecular forces) are only very weak; not much energy is needed to move the molecules further apart
Do not conduct electricity There are no ions present to carry the current

7. Giant Covalent Structures (C2.07)

Giant covalent structures (macromolecules, or giant molecular crystals) contain very many atoms held together by strong covalent bonds throughout the whole structure. Examples are the element carbon, as diamond and graphite, and compounds such as silica, SiO₂.

Diamond

  • Each carbon atom is covalently bonded to four others, arranged tetrahedrally. The strong bonds extend in all directions through the whole crystal.
  • Diamond has a very high melting point — a lot of energy is needed to break the many strong covalent bonds.
  • Diamond is very hard — it is the hardest natural substance, so it is used in cutting tools. The bonds are rigid, so diamond is much more brittle than a metal.
  • Diamond does not conduct electricity — all the outer electrons are used to form covalent bonds, so there are no electrons free to move. Diamond is a typical non-metallic element.

Graphite

  • The carbon atoms are arranged in flat layers of linked hexagons. Each layer is a two-dimensional giant molecule.
  • Within each layer, every carbon atom is bonded to three others by strong covalent bonds, so graphite also has a very high melting point.
  • Graphite is soft and slippery — between the layers there are only weaker forces of attraction, so the layers can slide over each other easily. It is used as a lubricant and in pencils, where thin layers of graphite are left on the paper.
  • Graphite conducts electricity — each carbon atom uses only three of its four outer electrons in covalent bonds, and the free electrons can move between the layers carrying charge, in a similar way to metals.

8. Metallic Bonding (C2.08)

The Structure of Metals

  • Metal atoms have relatively few electrons in their outer shells. When they are packed together, each metal atom loses its outer electrons into a 'sea' of delocalised electrons.
  • Having lost electrons, the atoms become positive ions (the number of protons in the nucleus is unchanged). A metal is therefore made of positive ions packed together and surrounded by electrons that can move freely between the ions.
  • The delocalised electrons are not restricted to orbiting one positive ion. They act as a kind of electrostatic 'glue' that holds the structure together.
  • Metallic bonding — the electrostatic force of attraction between the mobile 'sea' of electrons and the regular array of positive metal ions.

Physical Properties of Metals

Property Explanation using metallic bonding
Good conductors of electricity The mobile electrons can move through the structure, carrying the current; alloys such as solder and brass also conduct for the same reason
Malleable: easily bent and shaped The positive ions are arranged in layers. When a force is applied, the layers can slide over each other. The attractive forces act in all directions, so new bonds are easily formed and the metal is left with a new shape instead of breaking

10. Key Equations

Counting Subatomic Particles

  1. Number of protons = proton number (Z)
  2. Number of electrons in a neutral atom = number of protons = Z
  3. Mass number (A) = number of protons + number of neutrons
  4. Number of neutrons = A − Z
  5. Electrons in a positive ion = Z − charge, e.g. Na⁺ has 11 − 1 = 10 electrons
  6. Electrons in a negative ion = Z + charge, e.g. Cl⁻ has 17 + 1 = 18 electrons

Electronic Configuration and the Periodic Table

  1. Maximum number of electrons (first 20 elements): first shell 2, second shell 8, third shell 8
  2. Group number = number of electrons in the outer shell
  3. Period number = number of occupied shells
  4. Charge on a metal ion = number of outer electrons lost (Group I gives 1+, Group II gives 2+)
  5. Charge on a non-metal ion = number of electrons gained to fill the outer shell (Group VI gives 2−, Group VII gives 1−)

Forming Ions by Electron Transfer

  1. Na (2,8,1) → Na⁺ (2,8) + e⁻
  2. K (2,8,8,1) → K⁺ (2,8,8) + e⁻
  3. Mg (2,8,2) → Mg²⁺ (2,8) + 2e⁻
  4. Ca (2,8,8,2) → Ca²⁺ (2,8,8) + 2e⁻
  5. Cl (2,8,7) + e⁻ → Cl⁻ (2,8,8)
  6. O (2,6) + 2e⁻ → O²⁻ (2,8)

Activity C2.01 — Question 1

What was remarkable about the structure of the atom suggested by the Geiger and Marsden gold foil experiments carried out in Rutherford's laboratory?

Answer: Geiger and Marsden fired positively charged alpha particles at very thin gold foil. Activity C2.01 — Question 2

Their existence was suspected because atoms have more mass than their protons alone can explain, and James Chadwick finally detected them in 1932, indirectly, from their effect on other particles.

Question C2.03

Explain the terms atom and element, and include a clear description of the relationship between the two terms.

Answer: An atom is the smallest particle of an element that still shows the chemical characteristics of that element; it has a nucleus of protons and neutrons surrounded by electrons. An element is a substance that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical methods. The relationship: an element is made up of only one type of atom, because all the atoms of an element have the same number of protons (the same proton number). Question C2.04

Explain why neutrons are important in making the nucleus of an atom stable. You need to consider the charges on the different subatomic particles present.

Answer: The nucleus contains protons, which are all positively charged and packed very close together. Like charges repel, so the protons push each other apart. Neutrons have no charge, so they add to the nucleus without adding any repulsion; they separate the protons and help to hold the nucleus together. The more protons a nucleus has, the more neutrons it needs to stay stable: for example, gold has 79 protons and 118 neutrons. (Electrons are outside the nucleus, so they cannot do this job.) The only atom without neutrons is hydrogen, whose nucleus is a single proton with nothing to repel.

Question C2.06

By considering the subatomic particles present, explain why an atom of nitrogen is electrically neutral.

Answer: Nitrogen has proton number 7, so its nucleus contains 7 protons, each with a charge of +1 (total +7). The atom also has 7 electrons (arranged 2,5), each with a charge of −1 (total −7). The positive and negative charges are equal and opposite, so they cancel out: (+7) + (−7) = 0. The neutrons have no charge, so they do not change this. The atom therefore has no overall charge.

Practice Question 7

Carbon exists in two forms, diamond and graphite. Both forms have very high melting points. Graphite conducts electricity but diamond does not. (4 marks)

(a) Explain why graphite has a high melting point and conducts electricity. [2]

Answer:

(a) Graphite has a giant covalent structure in which each carbon atom is joined to three others by strong covalent bonds; a lot of energy is needed to break these many bonds, so the melting point is high. 13. Quick Revision Card

  • Element: one type of atom (same proton number); compound: elements chemically combined in fixed proportions; mixture: not chemically bonded and separated by physical methods.
  • Proton: mass 1, charge +1, in the nucleus. Neutron: mass 1, charge 0, in the nucleus. Electron: mass 1/1840, charge −1, in shells.
  • Z = number of protons = number of electrons (in an atom); A = protons + neutrons; neutrons = A − Z.
  • Shells hold 2, 8, 8: e.g. Na 2,8,1; Cl 2,8,7; Ca 2,8,8,2.
  • Group number = outer electrons; period number = number of shells.
  • Noble gases: full outer shells, very stable and unreactive; other atoms bond to reach this configuration.
  • Isotopes: same proton number, different numbers of neutrons; same chemical properties because they have the same electronic configuration.
  • Ionic bonding (metal + non-metal): electrons transferred; the metal forms positive ions and the non-metal negative ions, held by strong electrostatic attraction.
  • Ionic compounds: giant lattice; high melting and boiling points; often soluble in water; conduct only when molten or dissolved.
  • Covalent bond (non-metals): a shared pair of electrons; single (Cl–Cl), double (O=O, O=C=O) and triple (N≡N) bonds.
  • Simple molecules: strong covalent bonds within, weak intermolecular forces between, so low melting and boiling points; do not conduct.
  • Diamond: 4 bonds per carbon, tetrahedral; very hard, very high melting point, does not conduct; cutting tools and jewellery.
  • Graphite: 3 bonds per carbon in layers that slide, with free electrons; soft, used as a lubricant and in pencils, conducts (electrodes).
  • Metallic bonding: positive ions in a 'sea' of delocalised electrons; metals conduct electricity and are malleable.
